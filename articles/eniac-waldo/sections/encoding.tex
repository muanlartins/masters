\section{Encoding and Localization}
\label{sec:encoding}

Section~\ref{sec:enc-recog} fixes the binary encoding a weightless network reads and compares the tuned network against strong classical baselines on recognition; Section~\ref{sec:enc-loc} builds a weightless localizer and benchmarks it against a tuned random forest. Together they establish the weightless network as a competitive model on the task before the unlearning study.

\subsection{Encoding and recognition}
\label{sec:enc-recog}

A weightless network has no weights to compensate for a poor representation: each RAM memorizes the bit-tuples it saw for a class, and recognition is a vote over RAM lookups, so encoding the input is the modelling. We binarize each colour channel with a thermometer code, group the resulting position-tagged bits into RAM address tuples, and because the thermometer type and resolution and the tuple size all have interior optima, choose them by a grid search over three seeds rather than by default; the search settles on a Gaussian-spaced thermometer of 13 bits per channel with address size 12, which we fix for the comparison.

With the encoding fixed, we place the tuned weightless network beside logistic regression and a random forest, each tuned over its own hyperparameters on the raw pixels so that no baseline is a strawman. On clean patches the random forest leads at 0.95 macro-F1, the weightless network follows at 0.90, and logistic regression trails at 0.84; under full difficulty the three fall together to 0.77, 0.72, and 0.67. The weightless network is competitive on accuracy without dominating any axis: it is heavier (about 10~MB against the forest's 4.4~MB and logistic regression's 81~KB) and slower (about 1~ms per patch against the forest's 0.07~ms), so the benchmark does not secretly favour it.

\subsection{Localization}
\label{sec:enc-loc}

Localization needs a different formulation than regressing coordinates. A global Regression-WiSARD that reads the whole scene and predicts the target's centre fails, collapsing to the mean training position at 31~px error, no better than predicting that mean, because the random bit-sampling destroys spatial locality and a small target barely moves the scene's global statistics; position is a \emph{where}, not a \emph{what}. We instead slide a window across the scene, score each window for how well it is centred on the target with a Regression-WiSARD, and take the peak of the resulting score map as the predicted location.

To compare models rather than features, the weightless localizer and a random-forest window classifier read the same compact, hand-designed encoder; a random forest on raw pixels reaches 5.3~px, so the encoder helps it too and is reported as shared preprocessing. On a level field the weightless localizer ties the tuned forest: 3.8~px median error with 97\% of targets within 12~px, against the forest's 3.7~px and 99\%, at 540~KB against the forest's 1.6~MB, while a HOG+SVM detector trails at 19~px. Neither learner dominates the accuracy-versus-memory trade-off.

The tie is stable under difficulty. Retraining both models at each difficulty level, the weightless localizer is slightly ahead on clean scenes (3.1~px against 4.1~px) and the forest slightly ahead at the hardest setting (22.1~px against 23.6~px), with neither collapsing in between. Across recognition and localization the weightless network is therefore a competitive model on this task, which is the premise the next section depends on: the deletion advantage we measure is the advantage of a credible model, not of a weak one.
