\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Machine learning models trained on personal data inherit an obligation to forget it. Privacy regulations such as the Brazilian LGPD and the European GDPR grant individuals a right to erasure, and once a record has been absorbed into a trained model, removing it from storage is insufficient: the model must be restored to the state it would have reached without that record. This operation, \emph{machine unlearning}~\cite{caoyang2015,nguyen2022survey,xu2023survey}, also supports security, by excising a poisoned or backdoored example, and maintenance, by retiring stale or mislabeled data. A usable unlearning method must delete a record cheaply, completely, and verifiably.

These three requirements are hard to satisfy together. Retraining from scratch on the surviving data is complete and verifiable but pays the full training cost at every request; cheaper exact methods such as sharded retraining~\cite{bourtoule2021sisa} and data-removal-enabled forests~\cite{brophy2021dare} avoid that cost but still rebuild substructure or cache large statistics to stay exact, while approximate methods such as gradient ascent give up completeness, leaving residual traces that membership-inference attacks~\cite{shokri2017mia,chen2021mia} can recover. Accuracy benchmarks compound the problem, scoring only what a model predicts and never what it can forget, so a model's fitness for deletion never appears in the comparisons practitioners read. What the field lacks is the recognition that one model family already deletes by construction, together with a benchmark that treats deletion as a first-class property.

In principle, exactness is free for any model whose training decomposes additively over examples: if the trained state is the sum of independent per-example contributions, deleting an example is undoing its contribution, with no substructure to rebuild and no parameter to perturb. Weightless neural networks of the WiSARD family~\cite{aleksander1984wisard}, descended from the n-tuple recognizer~\cite{bledsoe1959ntuple}, are exactly such a model: each class is a set of memory nodes whose integer counters are incremented once per training example, independently and additively, so deleting an example decrements its counters and leaves the model bit-identical to one that never saw it. The wisardpkg library~\cite{carvalho2020wisardpkg} already exposes this operation, and additive models have long been known to admit exact unlearning in principle~\cite{caoyang2015}; what is missing is the recognition that this places weightless networks at an extreme of the unlearning trade space, and a benchmark that measures where. Measuring it needs a task with enough repeated, balanced identities to delete in volume and audit what remains. Where's Waldo, a visual-search puzzle with a recurring cast of look-alikes, is a convenient such task, and we build a synthetic, calibrated version of it because the few annotated real pages do not provide that volume~\cite{barthelmes2021wally}.

We locate weightless networks on the unlearning trade space using a synthetic benchmark and a shared evaluation harness. The generator emits per-identity-balanced instances with free localization labels, calibrated against real Where's-Waldo statistics; the harness runs WiSARD against four baselines (gold-standard retraining, sharded retraining, data-removal-enabled forests, and gradient ascent) on common data and splits, scoring retained accuracy, deletion cost, memory, and residual leakage by membership-inference AUC. Across class erasure, poison removal, deletion streams, and backdoor removal, WiSARD deletes a class in 40~ms against the retrained baseline's 1.3~s, at 9.3~MB of memory, with membership-inference AUC at chance and accuracy of 0.89 against the baseline's 0.95. The data-removal-enabled forest matches that 0.95 under exact deletion but spends 13.9~s and 1.6~GB to reach it, occupying the accuracy-first extreme of the trade space while WiSARD occupies the latency-first extreme (Figure~\ref{fig:trade}).

This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{(i)} a synthetic Where's-Waldo benchmark generator that produces per-identity-balanced instances with localization labels, calibrated against real Where's-Waldo statistics and released openly;
  \item \textbf{(ii)} the identification and first benchmark of weightless networks as an exact, residual-free unlearning substrate, evaluated against retraining, sharded retraining, data-removal-enabled forests, and gradient ascent, and audited by membership inference;
  \item \textbf{(iii)} an open evaluation harness implementing the four unlearning baselines and the membership-inference audit.
\end{itemize}

The paper is divided as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews weightless networks, the Where's-Waldo task, and the machine-unlearning literature. Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} motivates and describes the synthetic benchmark. Section~\ref{sec:encoding} establishes the weightless network as a competitive model on the task, fixing its input encoding and benchmarking a weightless localizer against a tuned random forest. Section~\ref{sec:unlearning} places the weightless network against the unlearning baselines. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} examines threats to validity, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
